# R2. Chess: do the larger Maia-3 models estimate a rating from one game better?

## Question

`chess_ratings/` estimates a Lichess blitz player's rating from one game with Maia3-5M. Do Maia3-23M and Maia3-79M, run through the same estimate unchanged, give a smaller mean absolute error on games that no model or choice here has seen? And how do all three compare with the baselines a reader would try first: guessing the mean rating, and guessing the opponent's rating?

## Why it matters

- The README's open questions ask whether a larger model would lower the error, and whether the floor is set by the model or by the noise in the rating itself (a settled rating wanders with a standard deviation of about 31 points; simulated in the folder).
- Maia-3's paper reports 55.4% move matching for Maia3-5M and 57.1% for Maia3-79M on its 2022 test games (cited in the README). Whether two points of move matching move a rating estimate is not known.
- A methods review of the folder found the README's comparison with "guessing the average" inflated by the stratified sample: on the population sample of the calibration test (600 player-games, August 2025), the mean guess is off by 281.7, the old method by 189.4, and the tempered likelihood with Lichess's rating distribution as prior by 163.0 (`results/calibration-test.json`). It also noted that the opponent's rating is observed in every game and is not used. This plan headlines the population sample and adds the opponent baseline.

## Data

- **Games.** Lichess rated blitz games from `Lichess/standard-chess-games` (Hugging Face; tagged cc0-1.0; database.lichess.org says that exports are released under CC0, read 2026-11-20). The Hugging Face copy holds months up to September 2025 (folder listing read 2026-11-20), and Maia-3 trained on January 2023 to July 2025.
- **Test.** August 2025, files 50 and 51 of 66. Earlier work read August files 40 and 41 (calibration test) and September files 30 to 40 and 50; none of those is used. The *population* sample is the first 600 qualifying games of file 51 in hash order (1,200 player-games), whatever their ratings. The *stratified* sample is 100 games in each of the five bands of the mean rating from file 50 (1,000 player-games), as in the calibration plan. Order is by md5(`chess-r2-test-2026-11-20` || Site).
- **Pilot.** September 2025, files 32 to 39, the calibration plan's pilot files, used here only to fit the secondary arm's temper τ for each model (see below) and to measure speed. 300 qualifying games in hash order with salt `chess-r2-pilot-2026-11-20`.
- **Who qualifies and which moves count,** as in `results/calibration-plan.md`: rated blitz, ended normally or on time, neither rating moved by more than 15 points, at least 40 half-moves, clock comments present; each player's moves before they first have under 30 seconds.
- **Usernames** are used inside the job for nothing and are not stored.

## Arms

One factor changes: the checkpoint. Everything else is the calibration study's code path (`calib-records`), with per-move log-probabilities on the grid 400, 500, …, 3000, both players given the same rating, and seven earlier positions.

- **Maia3-5M** (`UofTCSSLab/Maia3-5M`, the folder's model).
- **Maia3-23M** (`UofTCSSLab/Maia3-23M`).
- **Maia3-79M** (`UofTCSSLab/Maia3-79M`).

The checkpoint's SHA-256 and the `maia3` package commit (1e13597 unless an amendment changes it for all three) are recorded.

**Estimators.** The primary estimator is F0, the README's method: posterior mean under a flat prior on 600 to 2600, recomputed exactly from the wide grid. It has no fitted constant, so the models are compared with nothing tuned to any of them. The secondary estimator is F2+P: the likelihood raised to τ, with Lichess's rating distribution for the time control as prior. τ is fitted per model on the pilot games so that the 95% intervals cover 95%, as F2 was; for Maia3-5M the pilot fit is redone on the same pilot games so that all three are fitted alike.

**Baselines** (secondary, no model):

- **Mean guess:** the population pilot sample's mean rating.
- **Opponent guess:** the opponent's rating in the same game. Lichess pairs players of similar ratings, so this may beat every model. That would say the one-game estimate is a test of the move model, not a practical rating tool.

## Primary metrics and tests

Two primary hypotheses, with the Holm correction at a family level of 0.05, both on the population sample with F0:

- **H-79.** Mean absolute error of Maia3-79M minus that of Maia3-5M, paired by player-game.
- **H-23.** The same for Maia3-23M minus Maia3-5M.

95% intervals by bootstrap over games (both player-games of a game move together; 10,000 resamples, seed 0). The p-value for Holm is twice the share of resamples on the wrong side of 0. A larger model is said to estimate better if Holm rejects and the difference is below 0.

## Secondary metrics

Reported with intervals and without significance claims:

- The same differences under F2+P, and on the stratified sample.
- Mean error below 1400 on the stratified sample, for each model, under F0 and F2+P: does the bias that the calibration study traced to Maia-3's likelihood shrink with model size?
- 95% interval coverage (Wilson) and median width, per model and estimator.
- The mean guess and the opponent guess on both samples, and their differences from each model.
- Move matching at the players' own ratings, per model, on the same positions, as a check against the paper's 55.4% and 57.1%.
- **The floor.** The error of the best arm against the 31-point wander of a settled rating. If the best arm is above 150 points, the report says the model, not rating noise, sets the floor at this game length.
- **For context, not paired:** Finding Elo (Kaggle, 2014 to 2015), which asked for FIDE Elo from one game, with 25,000 training and 25,000 test games, scored by mean absolute error; the winning private score was 155.78 (leaderboard read 2026-11-20). Omori and Tadepalli (arXiv:2409.11506) report MAE 182 on Lichess games, as a methods review cites; not read for this plan. Both are other games and other rating systems.

## Minimum detectable effect

From the calibration test's population sample, an F0 error has a standard deviation near 160 points. The errors of two models on the same moves are expected to be strongly correlated; with a correlation of 0.8, a player-game's difference has a standard deviation near 100, and a game's mean of two near 85 (planning values, to be replaced by the pilot's). With 600 games, 2.80 × 85 / √600 ≈ 9.7 points at the two-sided 5% level, and 3.08 × 85 / √600 ≈ 10.7 at Holm's first level. The effect worth finding is 10 points, about 5% of F0's 189. If the pilot's standard deviation would put the detectable effect above 12 points, an amendment adds games from file 51 before any test game is read.

## Seeds

Test salt `chess-r2-test-2026-11-20`, pilot salt `chess-r2-pilot-2026-11-20`. Bootstrap seed 0. Maia-3 inference is deterministic given the checkpoint and device; the device is logged.

## Compute and cost

Hugging Face Jobs through `experiments/hfrun.py`, group `chess_ratings`, with `MAIA_DEVICE=cuda`. The weights are downloaded inside the job and never committed.

- **Queries.** About 32 counted moves per player-game (the README's median) × 27 grid points ≈ 864 queries. Test: 2,200 player-games, about 1.9 million queries per model. Pilot: 600 player-games, about 0.52 million per model.
- **Speed.** Maia3-5M answered about 1,750 queries a second on a T4 in the calibration study. The larger models' speed is not known; the plan assumes about 500 a second for 23M and 150 a second for 79M on a T4, and the pilot measures both.

| Job | Model | Queries | T4 hours at the assumed speed | Expected cost |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Speed pilot: 2,000 queries per model, and three positions checked against the UCI engine | all | 6,000 | 0.25 with setup | $0.10 |
| Pilot and test | 5M | 2.4 M | 0.4 | $0.16 |
| Pilot and test | 23M | 2.4 M | 1.6 | $0.64 |
| Pilot and test | 79M | 2.4 M | 4.7 | $1.88 |
| Estimates and scoring | | | `cpu-basic` | $0.01 |

**Expected about $2.80 on T4. Hard cap $5.00.** Each job's timeout is 1.5 times its estimate, set after the speed pilot. If the pilot shows that the 79M job would pass $3.00 on a T4, it moves to an L4 ($0.80 an hour) only if the pilot shows the L4 at least twice as fast, by an amendment before the main jobs. If no hardware fits the cap, 79M is scored on the population sample alone and the report says so.

## Outputs

- In git: `chess_ratings/results/r2.json` (per-band and overall numbers for each model and estimator, τ per model, the jobs and their costs) and a README section. No game or move is committed.
- In the private dataset, under `runs/r2/`: the per-move log-probabilities.

## Stopping rules and a null result

- The speed pilot must reproduce the authors' UCI engine's probabilities to seven decimals on three positions for each model, as the folder does for 5M. A model that fails is not run, and an amendment says why.
- The test games are read once per model, with F0 and F2+P frozen.
- **A null result.** If neither H-79 nor H-23 holds, the README states that the larger Maia-3 models did not estimate a rating from one game better than Maia3-5M on 600 August 2025 games, with each difference, its interval and the detectable effect. If the opponent guess beats every model, that is stated beside the model results, not after them.

## Threats to validity

- **Training overlap.** All three models trained on the same months, so August 2025 is outside every model's data.
- **One estimator held fixed.** F0 may suit one model's calibration better than another's. F2+P, refitted per model, is the check.
- **Blitz only,** and games of at least 40 half-moves.
- **The opponent's rating** is given to Maia-3 as R, not as the observed value, as in the folder's method. An estimate that conditions on the observed opponent rating is a different method and is left for a later plan.

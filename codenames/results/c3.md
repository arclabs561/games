# A giver trained against a population (plan C3)

Part of the [Codenames README](../README.md). The JSON files beside this note hold its numbers, with their seeds and settings.

**Findings.**

- Population training beats self-play with held-out embedding guessers by 0.31 words per turn (0.26 to 0.36), the plan's primary result, in all 70 runs.
- A rule with no training gets nearly all of that (exploratory, [below](#a-cautious-rule-with-no-training)). Always asking for one word, with the clue most likely to land on a team word, scores 0.30 (0.24 to 0.36) above self-play. The population policy's lead over the rule is 0.009 (−0.016 to 0.034), no detectable difference; the rule gets 97% (89% to 105%) of the gain. With the EAT guesser the rule is ahead by 0.020 (−0.017 to 0.058). What population training learned, on these guessers, is to ask for fewer words.
- The population giver breaks the printed clue rule more often. Its picks are a form of a board word, or share a compound part with one, on 16.6% (11.6% to 21.9%) of boards for the potion giver and 9.7% (6.0% to 13.8%) for the Qwen3 giver, against 3.5% (1.0% to 6.0%) and 1.2% (0.0% to 2.9%) for self-play ([diagnostics/legality.md](diagnostics/legality.md), exploratory). Every experiment filtered clues only with the substring rule of `words.legal`.
- On the AI competition's single-team score, which counts turns to find all nine words, the population giver is worse than self-play by 1.24 turns (0.48 to 2.00) with the framework's word-vector guessers. It asks for 1.04 words per clue against 1.78 ([bench.md](bench.md), exploratory, one training seed).
- Every guesser here is a model. C3's policies were not scored by language models or people. C11 scored C10's untrained population pick, a different giver, with two language models and found no detectable lead ([c11.md](c11.md)).

## The policy and the exploratory run

`rl.py` trains a policy over (clue, count) pairs: 30 shortlisted clues times counts 1 to 4. It picks an action with probability proportional to exp(θ · φ). The seven features φ use only the giver's own model: expected team words found, chance of hitting the assassin and an opponent word, the count, rarity, and the margin between the n-th closest team word and the closest other word. The reward is the exact expected value of the turn under a guesser drawn from a population. Because the reward of every action is known, the policy gradient is summed over all actions rather than sampled; boards and guessers are still sampled. The baseline is the policy's mean reward. Each run uses 300 training boards, 200 test boards and 400 Adam steps of 16 boards. One model is held out, and the population is the other six (all eight but the giver and the held-out one). The EAT association guesser is held out too.

**Exploratory run.** The table below is the first run, made while the code was written: two givers, potion with bge held out and Qwen3 with EmbeddingGemma 2 held out, with seed 0. The planned run after it repeats this with five seeds and every held-out choice.

| Giver, policy | own model | held-out model | EAT counts (held out) |
|---|---|---|---|
| potion, start: maximize expected words found | 1.24 ± 0.05 | 0.61 ± 0.06 (bge) | −0.06 ± 0.05 |
| potion, self-play | **1.60 ± 0.02** | 0.75 ± 0.04 | −0.05 ± 0.05 |
| potion, population | 1.03 ± 0.01 | **0.92 ± 0.02** | **0.35 ± 0.04** |
| potion, population, Deshpande et al. reward | 1.22 ± 0.05 | 0.61 ± 0.06 | −0.07 ± 0.05 |
| Qwen3 0.6B, start | 1.22 ± 0.05 | 0.26 ± 0.05 (Gemma 2) | −0.20 ± 0.05 |
| Qwen3 0.6B, self-play | **1.58 ± 0.03** | 0.43 ± 0.05 | −0.09 ± 0.05 |
| Qwen3 0.6B, population | 1.03 ± 0.01 | **0.73 ± 0.03** | **0.24 ± 0.04** |
| Qwen3 0.6B, population, Deshpande et al. reward | 1.28 ± 0.04 | 0.24 ± 0.05 | −0.17 ± 0.04 |

Values are expected turn values on the test boards, ± one standard error. Self-play learns to trust its own similarities. Its gap between own and held-out model is 0.85 for potion and 1.15 for Qwen3. Population play learns a cautious policy that weights the margin feature most and prefers lower counts. Its gap is 0.11 and 0.30. It does better than self-play with both held-out guessers, and only the population policies have a positive value against the association guesser. This is the pattern of other-play (Hu et al. 2020) and of Fictitious Co-Play (Strouse et al. 2021), whose design, training against a population and testing with held-out partners, is the nearer one: a policy trained with one partner exploits that partner's quirks. Here the quirks are the giver's own similarity scale and errors, not invented conventions, since the policy can only reweight seven features.

The last row uses the clue reward of Deshpande et al. (2026, their eq. 4): the fraction of team words the guesser names minus the fraction of other words it names, with the judge naming its n most likely words. That reward ignores the assassin and lets a turn continue past a wrong pick, so a policy trained on it gives more words per clue and scores worse on the game's own terms. This is one seed per giver; a run takes 1 to 5 minutes here, depending on load, and other choices are flags.

### Across seeds and held-out guessers (plan C3)

This run follows a plan written before it, `experiments/plans/C3.md`. It asks whether the exploratory result depends on the seed or on which model was held out. There are two givers, potion and Qwen3, and each of the other seven models is held out in turn, which makes 14 tasks. Each task is trained with seeds 1 to 5. All 70 runs are scored on the same 200 test boards, dealt from seed 3003, which no earlier run used. The primary metric is the held-out guesser's expected turn value, population policy minus self-play policy. It is paired by board and seed, averaged over boards and seeds within a task, then over the 14 tasks. Intervals are 95% stratified bootstrap intervals with 10,000 resamples (seed 0). Each resample draws the 200 boards once for all tasks and five seeds within each task, as Agarwal et al. (2021) do. The 14 tasks themselves are not resampled, so the interval is about these 14 choices.

The interval of the primary metric lies above 0. By the plan's rule, then, training against a population helps with held-out guessers of this kind. The gain is positive for every task, and each task's own interval lies above 0. The smallest is for the Qwen3 giver with harrier-oss-v1-0.6b held out, whose interval starts at 0.001; there self-play already scores 0.87. Seeds matter little. The difference varies between seeds of one task with a standard deviation of 0.016 on average, against 0.096 between tasks.

The learned weights are the same story in every run. In all 70 population policies the margin feature has the largest weight and the count a negative one. In all 70 self-play policies the expected number of words found has the largest weight. Population training with the Deshpande et al. reward scores no better than the untrained start with held-out guessers (0.34 against 0.35). Their game has no neutral words or assassin, so this is a test of their reward's transfer to this game, not a replication of their result.

**What changed from the exploratory run.** The direction holds and the size is similar. The two exploratory tasks were run again with five seeds and new test boards. For potion with bge held out, the gain is now 0.25 (0.15 to 0.35); in the exploratory run it was 0.92 − 0.75 = 0.17. For Qwen3 with EmbeddingGemma 2 held out, it is 0.36 (0.27 to 0.45), against 0.30 before. The exploratory gap between own and held-out value, 0.85 shrinking to 0.11 for potion, is now 0.91 shrinking to 0.14 on that task, and 1.15 shrinking to 0.34 on average over all 14 tasks. The cost in self-play, 0.57 in the exploratory run, is 0.50 on average. The plan names the held-out score as the headline rather than the gap, since a gap also shrinks when self-play gets worse.

The 70 runs ran on GitHub-hosted runners (CPU) through the workflow on the branch `compute/codenames-c3`, at commit `0dfb4c7`, one job per run, 70 to 130 seconds each and 1 hour 58 minutes in all; none failed. `compute/codenames-c3.yml` here is a copy of that workflow. The per-run outputs are on that branch under `runs/C3/44547011858-1/`. `results/c3.json` holds the summary, every θ, and the per-board values for the held-out and EAT guessers. One run was repeated off the runner, on a machine with two CPUs. It chose the same actions on every board, and its values differ from the runner's by at most 1.4e-5, from floating-point rounding.

## A cautious rule with no training

Exploratory: added after a review of C3, and not in its plan. On each test board, among the giver's 30 shortlisted clues, the rule gives the clue whose likeliest word is most likely a team word under the giver's own Luce guesser, always with count 1. A review ran it first on two tasks with seed 1, where it got 94% of the population's gain on one and beat the population policy on the other. The rule was then fixed and scored on all 14 tasks (`python -m codenames.cautious run`, `results/c3-cautious.json`). It needs no training seed, so each task's five population and self-play seeds are compared with the same rule values, with C3's stratified bootstrap.

| Measure, words per turn | Held-out embedding guesser | EAT guesser |
|---|---|---|
| Rule's mean value | 0.78 | 0.35 |
| Rule minus self-play | 0.302 (0.240 to 0.364) | 0.407 (0.327 to 0.488) |
| Population minus rule | 0.009 (−0.016 to 0.034) | −0.020 (−0.058 to 0.017) |
| Share of the population's gain that the rule gets | 97% (89% to 105%) | 105% (96% to 116%) |

The two rows add up to C3's primary result (0.31). In 2 of the 14 tasks the population's lead over the rule has an interval above 0 (0.048 for both givers with harrier-oss-v1-0.6b held out); in the other 12 it includes 0. The result says that the population policy's advantage over self-play, with these guessers, is the advantage of a count of one. It does not say whether a lower count helps with people, who can stop early; neither the rule nor the policies were scored by people. A confirmatory version would preregister the rule, and a count of at most 2, as baselines over all 14 tasks.
